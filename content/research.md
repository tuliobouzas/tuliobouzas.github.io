## Working papers

**New-Keynesian Open-Market Operations: A Reassessment of the Role of Money** [Job market paper] (2026).

{{< details 
    abstract="Quantitative New Keynesian models typically abstract from money. Using recent advances in New Keynesian modelling, this paper shows that money matters for the outcomes of monetary policy through the open market operations (OMO) that implement it. Empirically, I construct a new series of Federal Reserve permanent OMO from weekly reports (1979–2008) and show that open market operations respond significantly to high-frequency identified interest rate shocks within a month and, contrary to existing evidence, more strongly after the Fed began announcing its target rate. I build a heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian model with corporate debt and cash-in-advance constraints under which the central bank must conduct OMO to enforce its interest-rate target. The calibrated model matches the untargeted OMO response. Relative to a cashless benchmark, investment responds about 30\% more to a rate cut and household savings fall twice as much, because OMO finance investment through an inflation tax that falls hardest on poorer households. With money, deviations from the optimal Taylor rule are an order of magnitude costlier and output stabilization is unambiguously harmful, as consumption and hours respond strongly to inflation and OMO volume."

**The Price of Traceability: Tax Compliance, Currency Ratio, and Policy** [with Burak Uras] (2026).

{{< details 
    abstract="Why does cash remain prevalent despite the expansion of traceable electronic payments, and what are the implications for tax compliance and policy? Using randomized experimental evidence from Kenyan SMEs, we show that electronic-payment adoption increases tax compliance by improving transaction traceability. Motivated by this evidence, we develop and calibrate a general equilibrium model in which firms jointly choose method of payment acceptance, prices, and tax evasion. The model generates endogenous coexistence of cash and electronic payments and links tax compliance to the currency ratio, which is the use of cash at economic transactions relative to traceable payment instruments. A key result is that higher inflation raises the currency ratio and, when cash constrains firms’ ability to evade, it could increase tax evasion. We support this theoretical property with macroeconomic evidence on inflation, currency ratio, and tax compliance. The novel interactions we identify imply that monetary policy, tax policy, enforcement, and financial development jointly determine payment choices, tax compliance, and government revenues."

**On the Distributional Effects of Monetary Shocks and Market Incompleteness** (2025).


{{< details 
    abstract="I study the transmission of distortionary monetary policy shocks under incomplete markets. Using a heterogeneous agents general equilibrium model, I demonstrate that there is a unique fundamental stationary equilibrium, where the distribution of monetary holdings mirrors productivity, but infinite non-fundamental stationary equilibria for a given monetary base in the presence of a frictionless bonds market. Only financially constrained economies return to the fundamental stationary equilibrium after an unforeseeable monetary shock that redistributes monetary holdings, with aggregate effects on output and endogenous price stickiness along the transition. Financially developed economies display smaller distortions and negligible effects on aggregate variables, but monetary shocks create hysteresis by making the consequences of idiosyncratic shocks permanent. While partial market completion enhances welfare by enabling nearly perfect risk sharing, this improvement is limited by the irreversibility of the idiosyncratic shocks. Ultimately, distributional effects are irrelevant for monetary policy transmission to aggregate variables in developed economies but critical in poorer countries."

**Business Cycles and Informal Hiring with Misallocation of Capital**

{{< details 
    abstract="This paper builds a model of the role of informality along the business cycle in an economy with capital misallocation. The intensive margin is modelled through a size-dependent cost function; the extensive margin, as a separate, low-productivity informal sector whose firms cannot formalize. I find that the intensive margin gives less productive firms a competitive advantage during recessions: informal hiring becomes cheaper as firms shrink, and smaller firms are the most intensive in informal labor. As in prior literature, the intensive margin also absorbs shocks by lowering labor costs, though the effect is quantitatively small. In the calibrated model, eliminating either margin reduces total output. Both VAT and payroll taxes cause intersectorial misallocation, and the latter also favors small, informal-intensive formal firms over large ones."
